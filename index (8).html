What Your Liver Does

Your liver is the body's largest internal organ and its main processing plant — everything you eat, drink, or absorb passes through it.

Filters Blood

Processes everything you eat, drink, breathe, and absorb — removing toxins, drugs, and waste from your bloodstream.

Produces Bile

Creates bile to break down fats and absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K — essential for digestion.

Stores Energy

Converts excess glucose into glycogen, then releases it when your body needs energy between meals.

Makes Proteins

Produces albumin and clotting factors that keep fluid in your bloodstream and help wounds heal.

Regulates Cholesterol

Creates cholesterol and carrier proteins, balancing HDL and LDL levels in your body.

Processes Medications

Metabolizes most drugs you take — a big reason liver health affects how medicines work in your body.

Risk Factors

Some risks are lifestyle-driven and modifiable; others are genetic or viral. Knowing yours helps you decide when to get tested.

High Risk

Excess Alcohol

Regular heavy drinking is a leading cause of liver disease. Even moderate drinking adds strain over time.

High Risk

Obesity & Diabetes

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) affects roughly 1 in 4 adults globally and is often silent until advanced.

Moderate

Viral Hepatitis

Hepatitis B and C are major causes of cirrhosis and liver cancer. Vaccines exist for Hepatitis A and B.

Moderate

Family History

Genetic conditions like hemochromatosis or Wilson's disease raise risk — know your family health history.

Moderate

Certain Medications

Long-term use of some painkillers, antibiotics, and herbal supplements can affect the liver.

Protective

Regular Exercise

150 minutes of moderate exercise weekly is linked to notably lower fatty-liver risk. Movement is medicine.

Warning Signs

Liver disease is often called "silent" because symptoms appear late. Seek medical evaluation if you notice these signs.

Yellowing Skin or Eyes

Jaundice occurs when bilirubin builds up — one of the most recognizable signs of liver distress.

Abdominal Swelling

Fluid build-up (ascites) in the abdomen can signal cirrhosis or advanced liver disease.

Chronic Fatigue

Persistent tiredness that sleep doesn't fix — the liver's struggle to process toxins can drain your energy.

Dark Urine & Pale Stools

Tea-colored urine and clay-colored stools suggest bilirubin isn't being processed properly.

Itchy Skin

Bile salt build-up under the skin can cause persistent itching, especially on hands and feet.

Easy Bruising

The liver produces clotting factors — when damaged, you may bruise easily or bleed longer from cuts.

Liver-Friendly Diet

Food is one of the most powerful levers for liver health, especially for MASLD (fatty liver).

Stress, Cortisol & Carbohydrate Metabolism

Diet isn't only about what's on your plate — how your body handles stress changes how it processes the carbohydrates you eat, too. Here's the chain of events, in plain terms.

1

Stress triggers cortisol release. Physical or psychological stress prompts your adrenal glands to release cortisol, the body's main stress hormone.

↓
2

Cortisol tells the liver to make more glucose. It activates liver enzymes that break down stored glycogen (glycogenolysis) and build new glucose from other sources (gluconeogenesis) — a short-term "fight or flight" response that raises blood sugar so you have fuel on hand.

↓
3

Occasional stress is fine — chronic stress isn't. When cortisol stays elevated day after day rather than settling back down, blood sugar runs higher than it needs to, and the pancreas keeps releasing extra insulin to bring it back into range.

↓
4

Cells stop responding well to insulin. Persistently high cortisol and insulin can drive insulin resistance, meaning more sugar and insulin circulate in the blood without being used efficiently by muscle and fat tissue.

↓
5

The liver converts the excess into fat. Facing more sugar and insulin than it can use, the liver ramps up de novo lipogenesis (turning excess carbohydrate into fat), takes in more fat released from other tissues, and burns less fat for fuel — a combination that drives fat build-up inside the liver itself.

This is one reason researchers now study chronic, unmanaged stress as a contributing factor in MASLD (fatty liver), alongside diet, alcohol, and activity level. Managing stress — sleep, movement, and relaxation practices — is a liver-health lever too, not just what's on your plate.

Leafy Greens

Spinach, kale, and arugula are rich in glutathione — an antioxidant that supports the liver's detox pathways.

Cruciferous Vegetables

Broccoli, cauliflower, and Brussels sprouts support the liver's natural detox enzyme activity.

Fatty Fish

Salmon, mackerel, and sardines provide omega-3s that can help reduce liver fat and inflammation.

Nuts & Seeds

Walnuts and flaxseeds bring antioxidants and healthy fats that support liver enzyme activity.

Limit

Added Sugar

Fructose is metabolized almost exclusively by the liver — excess sugar is a key driver of fatty liver.

Limit

Processed Foods

Trans fats, preservatives, and high sodium make the liver work harder. Cook fresh meals where you can.

Exercise for Liver Health

Even without weight loss, exercise directly reduces liver fat. Aim for a mix of these three types.

Aerobic

Brisk walking, cycling, or swimming for 30 min, 5 days a week — the most consistently effective type for burning liver fat.

Resistance

Bodyweight or weights 2–3x weekly. Builds muscle mass, which improves insulin sensitivity and liver metabolism.

HIIT

Short bursts of intense activity — 15–20 min sessions, 2x weekly, are linked to meaningful drops in liver fat markers.

Start with what you can manage. A 10-minute walk after meals beats nothing. Consistency beats intensity — and check with your doctor before starting a new routine if you have an existing health condition.

Myths vs. Facts

Common misconceptions about liver health — and what the evidence actually says.

✕ Myth: "I can detox my liver with a juice cleanse."

Your liver detoxifies your body continuously on its own. There's no solid evidence that juice cleanses, detox teas, or liver flushes improve liver function — and some herbal "detox" products have actually been linked to liver injury.

✓ Fact: Coffee is linked to a healthier liver.

Multiple large studies associate 2–3 cups of coffee a day with lower rates of fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer, even in people with existing liver disease — both caffeinated and decaf appear to help.

✕ Myth: "Only heavy drinkers get liver disease."

MASLD (fatty liver linked to metabolic health) is now the most common liver condition worldwide, including in people who drink little or no alcohol. Obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome are major drivers.

✓ Fact: Early liver damage can be reversible.

Fatty liver and even early fibrosis can improve with weight loss, diet changes, and exercise. The liver can regenerate — but usually only if the underlying cause is addressed before cirrhosis sets in.

✕ Myth: "Natural supplements are always safe for the liver."

Herbal products like kava, comfrey, and high-dose green tea extract have been associated with liver injury. "Natural" doesn't automatically mean "safe," especially at high doses or combined with medication.

✓ Fact: Losing 5–10% of body weight can meaningfully help fatty liver.

Research consistently links modest weight loss — just 5–10% of body weight — to reduced liver fat, less inflammation, and even fibrosis improvement in people with MASLD.

Latest Research

A snapshot of recent, peer-reviewed developments in liver science. This is a research summary for awareness only, not a treatment recommendation — talk to your doctor about what applies to you.

Terminology · 2023–2026

NAFLD has been renamed MASLD

International experts introduced "metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease" (MASLD) to better reflect the metabolic roots of the condition. Diagnosis now centers on steatosis plus at least one metabolic risk factor — such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, or high cholesterol — regardless of alcohol intake, which has broadened how the condition is studied and treated.

UNIST, Pusan National University & Ulsan University Hospital · March 2026

A common B-vitamin shows promise against fatty liver

Researchers identified a microRNA (miR-93) that drives MASLD progression, and found that vitamin B3 (niacin) — an inexpensive, already-approved supplement — may help suppress it. It's early-stage research, not a proven treatment, but it points to a low-cost avenue worth watching.

UC San Diego · May 2026

New investigational drug targets MASH directly

Scientists identified a compound (ION224) designed to block a pathway involved in metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the more aggressive form of fatty liver disease that can progress to cirrhosis and liver cancer. It's in earlier-stage development, alongside other emerging MASLD/MASH drug candidates.

Lund University · February 2026

Gene-expression changes linked to liver fibrosis risk

An international team found altered expression of specific genes tied to liver fibrosis in people with obesity and MASLD, offering a possible new target for predicting or slowing disease progression. The team is now studying whether the link is causal.

Clinical guidance · 2026 update

Non-invasive testing is replacing routine biopsy

Updated clinical guidance increasingly favors non-invasive scoring tools — FIB-4, the ELF blood test, and elastography-based liver stiffness scans — over biopsy for staging fatty liver disease in most patients, making risk assessment more accessible.

These summaries are simplified for a general audience and may not reflect the full nuance of each study. Always check the original source and speak with a healthcare provider before changing any treatment.

Curious About Your Liver Fat? Try This Estimate

This tool gives an educational estimate based on a formula published in liver research, combining a few body measurements with two common blood-panel values. It takes under a minute if you have recent numbers on hand.

What these numbers mean: BMI (from height and weight) and waist circumference both reflect body fat, but waist circumference specifically flags abdominal fat, which is more closely tied to liver fat than BMI alone. GGT stands for gamma-glutamyl transferase, a liver enzyme reported on a standard blood/liver panel — it tends to rise when the liver is under strain. Triglycerides are a type of fat carried in your blood, and elevated levels are often associated with excess liver fat. The formula combines all four into a single 0–100 estimate.

This is not a diagnostic tool. It cannot diagnose fatty liver or any other condition. Whatever your result — lower, mixed, or higher — it should be verified by a doctor or other qualified healthcare professional, who can arrange proper testing to find out what's actually going on with your liver.

GGT and triglycerides come from a standard blood/liver panel. Not sure which unit your lab used for triglycerides? US labs usually report mg/dL; most labs in Australia, the UK, and the EU report mmol/L — check your results sheet if unsure.
