Curious About Your Liver Fat? Try This Estimate

This tool gives an educational estimate based on a formula published in liver research, combining a few body measurements with two common blood-panel values. It takes under a minute if you have recent numbers on hand.

What these numbers mean: BMI (from height and weight) and waist circumference both reflect body fat, but waist circumference specifically flags abdominal fat, which is more closely tied to liver fat than BMI alone. GGT stands for gamma-glutamyl transferase, a liver enzyme reported on a standard blood/liver panel — it tends to rise when the liver is under strain. Triglycerides are a type of fat carried in your blood, and elevated levels are often associated with excess liver fat. The formula combines all four into a single 0–100 estimate.

This is not a diagnostic tool. It cannot diagnose fatty liver or any other condition. Whatever your result — lower, mixed, or higher — it should be verified by a doctor or other qualified healthcare professional, who can arrange proper testing to find out what's actually going on with your liver.

GGT and triglycerides come from a standard blood/liver panel. Not sure which unit your lab used for triglycerides? US labs usually report mg/dL; most labs in Australia, the UK, and the EU report mmol/L — check your results sheet if unsure.

Want a Deeper Look? Try the FIB-4 Estimate

FIB-4 is a formula used in liver research that combines your age with three common blood-panel values to produce a single estimate. It's often used as a first-pass check before deciding whether more detailed liver testing is worth pursuing.

What these numbers mean: AST and ALT are liver enzymes reported on a standard blood/liver panel — both tend to rise when liver cells are under strain or being damaged. Your platelet count comes from a routine complete blood count (CBC); platelet levels can drop as liver scarring (fibrosis) becomes more advanced. The formula combines these with age because fibrosis becomes more common — and the formula less precise — later in life.

This is not a diagnostic tool. It cannot diagnose liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, or any other condition. Whatever your result — lower, mixed, or higher — it should be verified by a doctor or other qualified healthcare professional, who can arrange proper testing to find out what's actually going on with your liver.

Sources: cutoffs of 1.30 and 2.67 follow the AASLD practice guideline on blood-based liver assessments (2024); the 2.00 cutoff for ages 65+ follows McPherson et al., American Journal of Gastroenterology, 2017.

AST, ALT, and platelet count come from a standard blood panel and complete blood count (CBC) — your lab results sheet will have all three.

About This Site
LiverHealthHub is an independent educational project — it isn't a medical practice, clinic, or affiliated with any hospital or health system. It's maintained by one person, motivated by how common and how preventable most liver disease actually is, with the goal of turning scattered medical research into plain-language guidance people can actually use.

The content is researched and written by summarizing peer-reviewed studies and guidance from major health authorities (see the Research page for dated, sourced summaries and the citation behind the Liver Fat Estimate calculator) — it is not written or reviewed by a licensed medical professional, and nothing here should be treated as personalized medical advice.

Built with reference to how major health publishers (Mayo Clinic, Healthline, NHS, Liver UK) structure liver-health content: symptom guides, risk breakdowns, and dated, sourced research summaries.
