Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions we hear most. Tap a question to expand it.

What's the difference between fatty liver, MASLD, and MASH?

"Fatty liver" describes fat build-up in the liver. MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) is the current clinical term for fatty liver linked to metabolic risk factors like obesity, type 2 diabetes, or high cholesterol. MASH (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis) is the more advanced, inflamed form of MASLD that carries a higher risk of progressing to fibrosis or cirrhosis.

Can fatty liver actually be reversed?

In its early stages, yes — often within weeks to months. Weight loss of just 5–10% of body weight, reduced alcohol and added sugar intake, and regular exercise are all linked to measurable reductions in liver fat. Once significant scarring (fibrosis or cirrhosis) has developed, damage is generally no longer reversible, which is why early action matters.

How much alcohol is actually "safe" for my liver?

There's no universally safe amount — risk is a spectrum, not a cliff edge. U.S. clinical guidelines describe more than 14 drinks/week (men) or 7 drinks/week (women and adults 65+) as at-risk drinking. See the Alcohol & the Liver page for the full breakdown.

What blood tests actually check liver health?

The most common are ALT and AST (liver enzymes that rise with cell damage), GGT (rises with liver strain, alcohol use, and bile duct issues), and bilirubin (rises when the liver can't process waste products properly). These are usually bundled into a standard "liver function test" (LFT) panel your doctor can order.

Is the Liver Fat Estimate calculator on this site a diagnosis?

No. It's an educational estimate based on a published research formula, not a diagnostic tool. It cannot tell you whether you have fatty liver disease — only a doctor, using proper testing such as blood work or imaging, can do that. See the Risk Calculator page for the full disclaimer.

Are liver support supplements backed by real evidence?

It varies a lot by ingredient. Some, like vitamin E and omega-3s, have real clinical trial evidence in specific contexts, usually under medical supervision. Others, especially some herbal "detox" products, have weak evidence and in rare cases have themselves been linked to liver injury. See the Supplements and Medication Safety pages for specifics.

Can a medication damage my liver even if I'm taking it exactly as prescribed?

Yes, in rare cases — this is called drug-induced liver injury (DILI), and it can happen even at correct doses due to individual sensitivity. It's uncommon, which is why it's not a reason to stop necessary medication on your own, but it's also why doctors periodically order liver bloodwork for certain drugs.

When should I actually see a doctor about my liver?

See the When to See a Doctor page for a full decision guide — but as a rule, any yellowing of skin or eyes, dark urine, abdominal swelling, or unexplained easy bruising warrants prompt medical attention, not a wait-and-see approach.

These answers are simplified for general education and aren't a substitute for personalized medical advice. If something here concerns you, bring it to a doctor.

About This Site
LiverHealthHub is an independent educational project — it isn't a medical practice, clinic, or affiliated with any hospital or health system. It's maintained by one person, motivated by how common and how preventable most liver disease actually is, with the goal of turning scattered medical research into plain-language guidance people can actually use.

The content is researched and written by summarizing peer-reviewed studies and guidance from major health authorities (see the Research page for dated, sourced summaries and the citation behind the Liver Fat Estimate calculator) — it is not written or reviewed by a licensed medical professional, and nothing here should be treated as personalized medical advice.

Built with reference to how major health publishers (Mayo Clinic, Healthline, NHS, Liver UK) structure liver-health content: symptom guides, risk breakdowns, and dated, sourced research summaries.
