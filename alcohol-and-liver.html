Alcohol & Your Liver

Your liver processes almost all of the alcohol you drink — which is exactly why heavy or prolonged use affects it more than almost any other organ. Here's how the risk actually builds, and where the commonly cited safety thresholds come from.

~90%of the alcohol you drink is metabolized by the liver
>60g/daydaily intake at which fatty liver appears in about 9 in 10 drinkers
3 stagesfatty liver → alcoholic hepatitis → cirrhosis

How Alcohol-Related Liver Damage Progresses

1

Fatty liver (steatosis). Fat starts accumulating in liver cells. This stage is usually reversible with abstinence and typically causes no symptoms.

↓
2

Alcoholic hepatitis. Continued drinking triggers inflammation of liver tissue. Outcomes vary — from recovery with abstinence to progression toward liver failure in severe cases.

↓
3

Cirrhosis. Long-term inflammation leads to permanent scarring. At this stage damage is irreversible and can lead to portal hypertension and liver failure.

Clinical references note that daily intake of roughly 30–50g of alcohol (about 2–3.5 standard US drinks) sustained over 5+ years is associated with alcohol-related liver disease, with fatty liver appearing in about 90% of people drinking more than 60g/day.
At-Risk

Men

More than 14 drinks per week, or 4+ in a single sitting, is classed as at-risk drinking under U.S. clinical guidelines (NIAAA).

At-Risk

Women & Adults 65+

More than 7 drinks per week, or 3+ in a single sitting, is classed as at-risk drinking under the same guidelines.

Higher Risk

Alcohol + Metabolic Risk

Alcohol use combined with obesity, type 2 diabetes, or existing MASLD compounds liver risk more than either factor alone.

Reversible Early

The Early Stage Is Reversible

Fatty liver caused by alcohol typically improves within weeks of stopping or significantly cutting back — before scarring sets in.

Not a screening tool. These numbers describe population-level research thresholds, not a personal safety guarantee — liver sensitivity to alcohol varies by genetics, body weight, sex, and existing health conditions. If you're concerned about your own drinking or your liver, talk to a doctor — it's a practical, judgment-free conversation, and support is available if you want to cut back.
